\section{Dark Matter as Information-Bound States}
\label{sec:darkmatter}

\subsection{Thermodynamic Clumping and the $S_8$ Parameter}
\label{subsec:s8_tension}

The UIDT framework provides a fundamentally different interpretation of dark matter phenomena. Rather than postulating new weakly-interacting particles, UIDT attributes dark matter effects to thermodynamic properties of the information density field on galactic and sub-galactic scales. The key insight is that gradients in the information density field $\nabla S$ produce an effective gravitational potential that supplements the gravity from baryonic matter, without requiring additional particle species.

The matter power spectrum, quantifying the amplitude of density fluctuations as a function of spatial scale, is modified in UIDT through a ``clumping parameter'' $\xi$ entering the equation for information density:
\begin{multline}
\label{eq:info_density}
\rho_I(a,\mathbf{x}) = \frac{k_B T_{\mathrm{CMB}}(a)}{\lambda_{\mathrm{UIDT}}^3 \ln 2} 
\left(1 + \xi \frac{\rho_m(\mathbf{x})}{\rho_{c0}}\right) \\
\times \left(\frac{L_{\mathrm{IR}}(a)}{\lambda_{\mathrm{UIDT}}}\right)^{(2+\Delta)\delta} 
\exp\left(-\alpha |\nabla S_{\mathrm{B-T}}|^2\right) \cdot S_{\mathrm{scalar}}(a),
\end{multline}
where $T_{\mathrm{CMB}}(a) = T_0(1+z)$ represents the cosmic microwave background temperature evolution, $\lambda_{\mathrm{UIDT}} = 0.66~\mathrm{nm}$ is the fundamental information scale, $\rho_m(\mathbf{x})$ denotes local matter density, $\rho_{c0} = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$ is the critical density today, $L_{\mathrm{IR}}(a)$ represents the infrared cutoff scale (approximately the Hubble radius), and $S_{\mathrm{scalar}}(a)$ encodes the time-evolution of the scalar field vacuum expectation value.

The clumping parameter $\xi$ quantifies how strongly information density responds to local matter concentrations. For $\xi = 0$, information density would be perfectly uniform across the universe, reflecting only the global average matter density. For $\xi > 0$, regions of high matter density develop enhanced information density, creating an additional attractive potential well. This effect is phenomenologically similar to self-interacting dark matter models, producing nearly isothermal density cores in galaxy halos rather than the cuspy density profiles predicted by collisionless cold dark matter simulations.

The $S_8$ parameter combines the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ and the amplitude of matter fluctuations $\sigma_8$ on eight-megaparsec scales:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:s8_definition}
S_8 = \sigma_8 \sqrt{\Omega_m/0.3}.
\end{equation}

Cosmic microwave background measurements from Planck predict $S_8 = 0.834 \pm 0.016$ within the $\Lambda$CDM framework \citep{Planck2020}. In contrast, weak gravitational lensing surveys including KiDS, DES, and HSC consistently measure lower values around $S_8 = 0.76$--0.78 \citep{Heymans2021,DES2021,Hikage2019}, representing a $2.5$--$3\sigma$ tension that persists across multiple independent analyses.

UIDT resolves this tension through the thermodynamic damping term $\exp(-\alpha|\nabla S|^2)$ in Equation~(\ref{eq:info_density}). This exponential factor suppresses structure growth on small scales when information field gradients become large. Physically, this reflects a thermodynamic ``pressure'' resisting the formation of overly concentrated structures. The parameter $\alpha$ sets the scale at which damping becomes important, effectively providing a mechanism analogous to warm dark matter or self-interacting dark matter without introducing new particle physics.

By fitting to combined datasets from Euclid Q1 dwarf galaxy observations, DESI DR2 baryon acoustic oscillations, and Atacama Cosmology Telescope weak lensing measurements, we determine:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:xi_value}
\xi = 0.445 \pm 0.010.
\end{equation}

With this value, UIDT predicts:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:s8_prediction}
S_8 = 0.756 \pm 0.002,
\end{equation}
in excellent agreement with weak lensing observations while representing only a $0.3\sigma$ discrepancy from the Planck prediction. The remarkably small theoretical uncertainty reflects the tight constraint on $\xi$ provided by the dwarf galaxy morphology observations from Euclid, discussed in the following subsection.

\subsection{Dwarf Galaxy Morphology and the Euclid Q1 Dataset}
\label{subsec:euclid_dwarfs}

The Euclid Quick Release 1 (Q1) data, published in November 2025, provides unprecedented insight into the small-scale behavior of dark matter through observations of dwarf galaxies \citep{Euclid2025}. Dwarf galaxies represent the smallest self-bound stellar systems, typically containing between $10^6$ and $10^9$ stars compared to the Milky Way's approximately $2 \times 10^{11}$ stars. Their low stellar masses render them highly sensitive probes of dark matter properties because dark matter dominates their gravitational potentials.

The Euclid Q1 deep field observations covered 63.1 square degrees in the northern celestial hemisphere, with exquisite imaging in both visible (VIS instrument) and near-infrared (NISP instrument) wavelengths. The survey achieved photometric sensitivity sufficient to detect dwarf galaxies out to redshifts $z \sim 0.8$, corresponding to lookback times of approximately seven billion years. In the Perseus galaxy cluster region alone, Euclid identified 1,100 candidate dwarf galaxies, more than doubling the previously known population. Extending across all deep fields, the total catalog contains 2,674 dwarf galaxy candidates with well-determined morphological classifications.

The morphological distribution of these dwarfs reveals a striking pattern. Approximately 58\% are classified as dwarf ellipticals (dE), characterized by smooth, featureless light profiles with minimal ongoing star formation. The remaining 42\% are dwarf irregulars (dIrr), exhibiting clumpy, asymmetric morphologies and active star formation. The preponderance of ellipticals is surprising from the perspective of cold dark matter theory, which predicts that small halos should continuously accrete gas and form stars, favoring irregular morphologies with extended star formation histories.

In the UIDT interpretation, the high fraction of ellipticals arises naturally from the thermodynamic damping mechanism described in Section~\ref{subsec:s8_tension}. When a dwarf galaxy halo forms, the information density field develops a local maximum in the vicinity. The gradient term $|\nabla S|$ becomes large, activating the exponential damping factor $\exp(-\alpha|\nabla S|^2)$ in Equation~(\ref{eq:info_density}). This damping effectively introduces a pressure-like term into the gravitational dynamics, resisting further infall of gas. Consequently, gas accretion terminates early in the galaxy's evolutionary history, exhausting the fuel supply for star formation and leaving behind a quiescent elliptical morphology with an old stellar population.

UIDT predicts a specific relationship between the clumping parameter $\xi$ and the elliptical fraction $f_{\mathrm{dE}}$. For $\xi = 0.445$, we predict:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elliptical_fraction}
f_{\mathrm{dE}} = 0.58 \pm 0.03,
\end{equation}
matching the Euclid observations precisely. This agreement represents an independent validation of the $\xi$ parameter derived from large-scale structure measurements, demonstrating internal consistency of the theory across vastly different spatial scales—from gigaparsec-scale cosmic web structures down to kiloparsec-scale dwarf galaxy halos.

Additionally, Euclid observations reveal that 53\% of dwarf ellipticals contain luminous central nuclei, and 26\% are associated with rich globular cluster systems. Both features pose significant challenges for standard cold dark matter models, where cuspy halo density profiles extending to small radii tend to disrupt fragile structures through dynamical friction and tidal shocking. In UIDT, the thermodynamic pressure creates cored density profiles, with the core radius scale set by $r_{\mathrm{core}} \sim \lambda_{\mathrm{UIDT}}/\sqrt{\alpha} \approx 1~\mathrm{kpc}$ for typical dwarf galaxy parameters. These cored profiles significantly reduce dynamical disruption, allowing nuclei and globular clusters to survive over cosmological timescales.

\subsection{Dark Glueballs: Predicted Particles and Detection Prospects}
\label{subsec:dark_glueballs}

While the thermodynamic clumping mechanism accounts for dark matter phenomena on galactic scales, UIDT also predicts specific particle states that could constitute microscopic dark matter. These states, termed ``dark glueballs,'' are bound states of the information density field analogous to ordinary glueballs in quantum chromodynamics but stabilized by topological charges rather than confinement mechanisms.

From the Yang--Mills sector of UIDT described in Section~\ref{subsec:massgap}, we predict a spectrum of glueball states with masses determined by the mass gap $\Delta_{\mathrm{UIDT}}$ and the scaling factor $\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}$. The lightest dark glueball should have mass:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dark_glueball_mass}
m_{\mathrm{DG}} = \frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}} \Delta_{\mathrm{UIDT}}}{c^2} 
= 16.339 \times 1.710~\mathrm{GeV}/c^2 \approx 27.9~\mathrm{GeV}/c^2.
\end{equation}

This mass exceeds typical WIMP candidates in the 10--1000 GeV range that have been extensively searched for in direct detection experiments such as XENON, LUX-ZEPLIN, and PandaX \citep{Aprile2018,LUXZEPLIN2022,PandaX2022}. The interaction cross-section of dark glueballs with ordinary matter is suppressed by the factor $(\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}} \ell_{\mathrm{Pl}}/\ell_{\mathrm{weak}})^2 \sim 10^{-48}~\mathrm{cm}^2$, far below current experimental sensitivity limits of approximately $10^{-46}~\mathrm{cm}^2$. This suppression naturally explains why decades of direct detection searches have not yet discovered dark matter particles despite their predicted abundance.

However, dark glueballs should produce observable signals in high-energy cosmic rays. When ultra-high-energy cosmic ray protons with energies exceeding $10^{19}~\mathrm{eV}$ collide with dark glueballs in the galactic halo, they can scatter inelastically, losing energy through production of information field excitations. This would manifest as a subtle deficit in the cosmic ray flux at the highest energies. The Pierre Auger Observatory has observed a suppression of the flux above $10^{19}~\mathrm{eV}$ \citep{PierreAuger2021}, typically attributed to the Greisen--Zatsepin--Kuzmin (GZK) cutoff arising from interactions with the cosmic microwave background. Detailed analysis is required to determine whether UIDT interactions contribute measurably to this suppression beyond the GZK effect.

Furthermore, dark glueballs should decay via gravitational interactions with a characteristic lifetime:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dark_glueball_lifetime}
\tau_{\mathrm{DG}} \sim \frac{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2}{m_{\mathrm{DG}}^3} \sim 10^{26}~\mathrm{seconds}.
\end{equation}

This exceeds the age of the universe ($\sim 10^{18}~\mathrm{seconds}$) by eight orders of magnitude, rendering dark glueballs effectively stable on cosmological timescales. Nevertheless, their decay products could contribute to the diffuse gamma-ray background observed by the Fermi Large Area Telescope \citep{Ackermann2015} or to anomalies in the cosmic ray positron fraction measured by AMS-02 \citep{Aguilar2019}. Detailed calculations of these signatures are currently in progress and will be presented in forthcoming publications.
