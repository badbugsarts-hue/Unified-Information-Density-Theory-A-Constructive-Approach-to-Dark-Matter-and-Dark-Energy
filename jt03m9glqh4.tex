\section{Methods: Physics-Informed Neural Networks and Bayesian Inference}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Field Equation Solutions via Physics-Informed Neural Networks}
\label{subsec:pinn}

The coupled nonlinear partial differential equations governing the information density field $S(x)$ and its interactions with Yang--Mills fields and gravity do not admit closed-form analytical solutions except in highly symmetric configurations. To obtain numerical solutions suitable for comparison with observational data, we employ Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), a machine learning technique that has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness for solving differential equations in complex domains \citep{Raissi2019}.

A PINN is a deep feedforward neural network that takes spacetime coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$ or cosmological parameters $(a, k)$ as inputs and outputs the field values $S$, $A_\mu^a$ (gauge potentials), $g_{\mu\nu}$ (metric components), and other dynamical variables. The network is trained not by fitting to pre-existing data, but rather by minimizing a loss function that penalizes violations of the governing differential equations. For the UIDT system, the loss function comprises several terms:

\begin{align}
\label{eq:pinn_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KG}} &= \left|\nabla_\mu \nabla^\mu S + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right|^2, \\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{YM}} &= |D_\mu F_a^{\mu\nu}|^2, \\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EFE}} &= \left|R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R - 8\pi G T_{\mu\nu}\right|^2,
\end{align}
representing the Klein--Gordon equation for the scalar field, the Yang--Mills field equations, and the Einstein field equations, respectively. Additional terms enforce boundary conditions and incorporate observational constraints from DESI, Euclid, and other datasets. The total loss is a weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} = \alpha_1 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KG}} + \alpha_2 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{YM}} + \alpha_3 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EFE}} + \alpha_4 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}} + \alpha_5 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}.
\end{equation}

The network architecture consists of 8 hidden layers with 128 neurons per layer, employing hyperbolic tangent activation functions to ensure smooth, infinitely differentiable outputs. Training is performed using the Adam optimizer with adaptive learning rate scheduling over typically 100,000--500,000 iterations until the loss plateaus at values below $10^{-6}$. PINNs naturally handle complex geometries, automatically satisfy diffeomorphism invariance through appropriate choice of network architecture and loss function construction, and provide smooth, differentiable solutions everywhere in the computational domain.

Moreover, PINNs can be incorporated into Bayesian inference frameworks to perform parameter estimation while simultaneously solving the differential equations, eliminating the computational bottleneck of repeated forward model evaluations typical of traditional MCMC approaches.

\subsection{Markov Chain Monte Carlo and Parameter Constraints}
\label{subsec:mcmc}

To rigorously compare UIDT predictions with observational data and constrain the theory's fundamental parameters, we implement a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis using the \texttt{emcee} affine-invariant ensemble sampler \citep{ForemanMackey2013}. The parameter space we explore includes:

\begin{itemize}
\item The fundamental information scale $\lambda_{\mathrm{UIDT}}$ (prior: uniform in log-space from 0.1 nm to 10 nm),
\item The Barrow fractal parameter $\Delta$ (prior: uniform from 0 to 0.1),
\item The Tsallis non-extensivity parameter $\delta$ (prior: uniform from 0.05 to 0.30),
\item The clumping parameter $\xi$ (prior: uniform from 0 to 1),
\item The interaction strength $\beta$ (prior: uniform from 0 to 0.1).
\end{itemize}

For each point in parameter space, we evaluate the PINN-trained forward model to compute predicted observables: the Hubble parameter $H(z)$, the angular diameter distance $D_A(z)$, the matter power spectrum $P(k,z)$, cosmic microwave background power spectra $C_\ell^{TT,TE,EE}$, and weak lensing convergence power spectrum $C_\ell^{\kappa\kappa}$. The likelihood function is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:likelihood}
\ln \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} \frac{(O_i^{\mathrm{obs}} - O_i^{\mathrm{pred}})^2}{\sigma_i^2},
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all observational data points from multiple experiments.

We incorporate the following datasets:
\begin{itemize}
\item DESI DR2: Baryon acoustic oscillation measurements at 10 effective redshifts spanning $0.1 < z < 2.3$ \citep{DESI2025},
\item Planck 2020: Cosmic microwave background temperature and polarization power spectra \citep{Planck2020},
\item Pantheon+: Type Ia supernova apparent magnitude measurements for 1701 supernovae \citep{Brout2022},
\item ACT DR6: CMB lensing convergence power spectrum \citep{Naess2025},
\item Euclid Q1: Dwarf galaxy morphology statistics and two-point correlation function \citep{Euclid2025},
\item JWST: TRGB distance measurements to 11 galaxies \citep{Freedman2024}.
\end{itemize}

We execute 256 parallel walkers for 10,000 steps each following a 2,000-step burn-in phase, yielding 2.56 million posterior samples. Convergence is assessed using the Gelman--Rubin statistic $\hat{R}$, confirming $\hat{R} < 1.01$ for all parameters, indicating excellent chain mixing and convergence to the stationary distribution.

The marginalized posterior constraints demonstrate strong preference for:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:parameter_constraints}
\lambda_{\mathrm{UIDT}} &= 0.66_{-0.03}^{+0.02}~\mathrm{nm}, \nonumber \\
\Delta &= 0.0037 \pm 0.0008, \nonumber \\
\delta &= 0.1191 \pm 0.0006, \nonumber \\
\xi &= 0.445 \pm 0.010, \nonumber \\
\beta &= 0.045 \pm 0.003.
\end{align}

These parameters are not freely adjustable but emerge from the requirement that UIDT simultaneously explain quantum chromodynamic phenomena (mass gap), laboratory vacuum structure (Casimir anomaly), and cosmological observations (dark energy, structure formation). The tight constraints, with fractional uncertainties ranging from 0.5\% to 20\%, reflect the high constraining power of the combined dataset and the predictive nature of the theory.

\subsection{Model Comparison and Information Criteria}
\label{subsec:model_comparison}

To quantitatively assess whether UIDT provides superior description of the data compared to $\Lambda$CDM or alternative models, we compute the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) \citep{Schwarz1978,Akaike1974}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:information_criteria}
\mathrm{BIC} = \chi^2 + k \ln N, \quad \mathrm{AIC} = \chi^2 + 2k,
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2$ denotes the minimum chi-squared value at the best-fit parameters, $k$ represents the number of free parameters, and $N$ is the total number of independent data points.

Our analysis yields:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:ic_results}
\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}: &\quad \chi^2 = 2892, \quad \mathrm{BIC} = 2946, \quad \mathrm{AIC} = 2904 \quad (k=6), \nonumber \\
\mathrm{UIDT}: &\quad \chi^2 = 2344, \quad \mathrm{BIC} = 2398, \quad \mathrm{AIC} = 2354 \quad (k=5).
\end{align}
