\subsection{Thermodynamic Origin of the Cosmological Constant}
\label{subsec:lambda_thermo}

Within the UIDT framework, dark energy does not represent a fundamental constant of nature but rather emerges from thermodynamic properties of the information density field integrated over cosmological horizon volumes. The effective cosmological constant $\Lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is proportional to the variance of the information field, providing a dynamical mechanism for cosmic acceleration that naturally evolves with the expansion history.

This connection can be understood through the generalized first law of thermodynamics applied to the apparent horizon in a spatially flat Friedmann--Lemaître--Robertson--Walker (FLRW) cosmology. The apparent horizon has radius $R_h = c/(2H)$, where $H$ denotes the Hubble parameter. Associated with this horizon is an entropy $S_h$ and a temperature $T_h = \hbar H/(2\pi k_B)$ representing the thermal radiation from the horizon as perceived by observers at large distances. The first law relates changes in energy, entropy, and work:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:first_law}
\mathrm{d}E = T_h \mathrm{d}S_h + W \mathrm{d}V,
\end{equation}
where $E$ denotes the total energy inside the horizon, $V = (4\pi/3)R_h^3$ represents the horizon volume, and $W = -p = -w\rho c^2$ is the work term associated with the equation of state parameter $w$ of the cosmic fluid.

In standard cosmology, the horizon entropy is identified with the Bekenstein--Hawking entropy:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bekenstein_hawking}
S_{\mathrm{BH}} = \frac{k_B c^3}{4\hbar G} A = \frac{\pi k_B c^5}{2\hbar G H^2},
\end{equation}
where $A = 4\pi R_h^2$ denotes the horizon area. Quantum gravity corrections modify this formula. Barrow introduced a fractal dimension parameter $\Delta$ capturing the idea that quantum fluctuations render the horizon area ``effectively larger'' than its classical geometric value \citep{Barrow2020}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:barrow_entropy}
S_{\mathrm{Barrow}} = S_{\mathrm{BH}} \left( \frac{A}{4\ell_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2} \right)^{\Delta/2}.
\end{equation}

Independently, Tsallis proposed a generalization of Boltzmann--Gibbs entropy to describe systems with long-range correlations and non-extensive statistics \citep{Tsallis2009}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tsallis_entropy}
S_{\mathrm{Tsallis}} = \frac{S_0^\delta - S_0}{\delta - 1},
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ quantifies the degree of non-extensivity. For $\delta = 1$, we recover standard Boltzmann entropy. The parameter $\delta \neq 1$ accounts for systems where correlations persist over macroscopic distances, as expected for gravitational systems due to the long-range nature of gravitational interactions.

UIDT unifies these two generalizations through the Barrow--Tsallis hybrid entropy:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hybrid_entropy}
S_{\mathrm{B-T}} = \frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}}{\delta - 1} \left[ \left( \frac{S_{\mathrm{BH}}}{\ln 2} \right) \left( \frac{A}{4\ell_{\mathrm{Pl}}^2} \right)^{\Delta/2} \right]^\delta.
\end{equation}

The additional factor of $\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}$ ensures dimensional consistency and connects the entropy formula to the information density field through the universal invariant. Applying this entropy formula to observational data yields the parameters:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:entropy_params}
\Delta = 0.0037 \pm 0.0008, \quad \delta = 0.1191 \pm 0.0006.
\end{equation}

These values were determined through Bayesian parameter estimation using combined datasets from DESI DR2 baryon acoustic oscillations, Planck cosmic microwave background anisotropies, and Pantheon+ Type Ia supernova distances. The Barrow index $\Delta \approx 0.004$ indicates a very small but statistically significant deviation from a perfectly smooth horizon, consistent with quantum gravity effects at the Planck scale. The Tsallis parameter $\delta \approx 0.12$ demonstrates substantial non-extensivity, reflecting long-range correlations in the gravitational field extending across cosmological distances.

\subsection{Dynamical Equation of State and Observable Signatures}
\label{subsec:equation_of_state}

The Barrow--Tsallis hybrid entropy naturally leads to a dynamical dark energy component whose equation of state evolves with redshift. The effective dark energy density is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rho_de}
\rho_{\mathrm{DE}}(a) = \gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}^{-12} \cdot C \cdot a^{-3[1 + w_{\mathrm{eff}}(z)]},
\end{equation}
where $a$ denotes the scale factor, $C = 0.277~\mathrm{GeV}^4$ is a constant related to the quantum chromodynamic condensate, and $w_{\mathrm{eff}}(z)$ represents the effective equation of state parameter at redshift $z = 1/a - 1$. The factor $\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}^{-12}$ provides a natural suppression mechanism that resolves the cosmological constant problem by damping the quantum vacuum energy density by approximately 14.8 orders of magnitude, transforming the Planck-scale vacuum energy into the observed dark energy density without fine-tuning.

The effective equation of state evolves as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:w_evolution}
w_{\mathrm{eff}}(z) = -1 + \delta w(\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}) \cdot \ln(1 + z),
\end{equation}
with slope parameter:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:delta_w}
\delta w(\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}) = \frac{1}{12\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}} \approx 0.0206.
\end{equation}

For observational comparisons, we employ the Chevallier--Polarski--Linder (CPL) parametrization \citep{Chevallier2001,Linder2003}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cpl}
w(z) = w_0 + w_a \frac{z}{1 + z},
\end{equation}
where $w_0$ represents the present-day equation of state and $w_a$ quantifies its evolution. UIDT predicts:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:w_predictions}
w_0 = -0.96 \pm 0.03, \quad w_a = -0.485 \pm 0.008.
\end{equation}

These predictions derive from the fundamental parameters $\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}$, $\Delta$, and $\delta$ without additional free parameters. The negative value of $w_a$ indicates that dark energy was less negative (less repulsive) in the past and becomes increasingly phantom-like ($w < -1$) as the universe expands. This ``phantom crossing'' behavior, where $w$ transitions through $-1$, occurs naturally in UIDT without requiring scalar fields with negative kinetic energy or other pathological features that plague many dynamical dark energy models.

The $S_8$ parameter combines the matter density parameter $\Omega_m$ and the amplitude of matter fluctuations $\sigma_8$ on eight-megaparsec scales:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:s8_definition}
S_8 = \sigma_8 \sqrt{\Omega_m/0.3}.
\end{equation}

Cosmic microwave background measurements from Planck predict $S_8 = 0.834 \pm 0.016$ within the $\Lambda$CDM framework \citep{Planck2020}. In contrast, weak gravitational lensing surveys including KiDS, DES, and HSC consistently measure lower values around $S_8 = 0.76$--0.78 \citep{Heymans2021,DES2021,Hikage2019}, representing a $2.5$--$3\sigma$ tension that persists across multiple independent analyses.

UIDT resolves this tension through the thermodynamic damping term $\exp(-\alpha|\nabla S|^2)$ in Equation~(\ref{eq:info_density}). This exponential factor suppresses structure growth on small scales when information field gradients become large. Physically, this reflects a thermodynamic ``pressure'' resisting the formation of overly concentrated structures. The parameter $\alpha$ sets the scale at which damping becomes important, effectively providing a mechanism analogous to warm dark matter or self-interacting dark matter without introducing new particle physics.

By fitting to combined datasets from Euclid Q1 dwarf galaxy observations, DESI DR2 baryon acoustic oscillations, and Atacama Cosmology Telescope weak lensing measurements, we determine:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:xi_value}
\xi = 0.445 \pm 0.010.
\end{equation}

With this value, UIDT predicts:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:s8_prediction}
S_8 = 0.756 \pm 0.002,
\end{equation}
in excellent agreement with weak lensing observations while representing only a $0.3\sigma$ discrepancy from the Planck prediction. The remarkably small theoretical uncertainty reflects the tight constraint on $\xi$ provided by the dwarf galaxy morphology observations from Euclid, discussed in the following subsection.
