\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The profound mysteries of dark matter and dark energy represent not merely observational puzzles but fundamental challenges to our understanding of physical reality. Dark energy, first revealed through Type Ia supernova observations that demonstrated the accelerated expansion of the universe \citep{Riess1998,Perlmutter1999}, dominates the cosmic energy budget with approximately 68\% of the total mass-energy content. Within the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmological framework, this component is traditionally modeled as a cosmological constant $\Lambda$ characterized by an equation of state parameter $w \equiv p/\rho = -1$, where $p$ represents pressure and $\rho$ denotes energy density. While this minimal description has achieved remarkable empirical success in fitting observational data from the cosmic microwave background, large-scale structure surveys, and standardizable distance indicators \citep{Planck2020}, recent high-precision measurements have begun to reveal tantalizing hints of more complex behavior.

Dark matter, comprising approximately 27\% of the universe's mass-energy content, manifests through its gravitational effects on galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing phenomena, and the formation of cosmic structure \citep{Clowe2006}, yet it steadfastly evades direct detection in terrestrial experiments. Despite decades of intensive searches, no definitive particle candidate has emerged from experiments designed to detect weakly interacting massive particles, axions, or other hypothetical constituents \citep{Aprile2018}. The simultaneous presence of these two mysterious components—together accounting for 95\% of the universe's energy content—suggests that our theoretical edifice requires fundamental reconstruction rather than incremental modification.

Recent precision measurements from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Data Release~2, published in 2025, have revealed compelling evidence that dark energy may exhibit dynamical evolution rather than behaving as a pure cosmological constant \citep{DESI2025}. Analysis of baryon acoustic oscillation measurements spanning 14.3 million spectroscopically confirmed galaxies and quasars across the redshift range $0.1 < z < 2.3$ demonstrates a statistical preference at the $2.8\sigma$ to $6.4\sigma$ level (depending on dataset combinations) for a time-varying equation of state. This finding, if confirmed by independent observations, would necessitate a fundamental revision of our theoretical understanding of cosmic acceleration.

Simultaneously, the cosmological concordance model faces increasingly severe internal tensions. The Hubble tension—a persistent $5\sigma$ discrepancy between the Hubble constant $H_0$ measured from early universe cosmic microwave background observations ($67.4 \pm 0.5~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ from Planck; \citealt{Planck2020}) and late universe distance ladder measurements ($73.04 \pm 1.04~\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ from SH0ES; \citealt{Riess2022})—has resisted resolution for over a decade despite intensive scrutiny of systematic uncertainties. The $S_8$ tension, quantifying a similar discrepancy between cosmic microwave background predictions and weak gravitational lensing measurements of matter clustering amplitude \citep{Heymans2021,DES2021,Hikage2019}, adds further strain to the theoretical framework. These tensions may indicate not merely calibration challenges or unaccounted systematics, but rather fundamental incompleteness in our theoretical description of cosmic evolution.

The Unified Information-Density Theory (UIDT) offers a radically different paradigm for addressing these observational challenges. Rather than treating information as an abstract mathematical descriptor of physical states—a mere bookkeeping device for tracking knowledge about systems—UIDT proposes that information density constitutes the fundamental ontological entity, with conventional notions of mass, energy, and spacetime geometry emerging as derived, effective descriptions. This framework centers on the information density field $S(x)$, a Lorentz-covariant scalar field with mass dimension one, which serves as the primary degree of freedom from which all physical phenomena derive through appropriate scaling relations.

Crucially, the field $S(x)$ couples non-perturbatively to the Yang--Mills gauge field of quantum chromodynamics, providing what we demonstrate to be a constructive solution to the Yang--Mills existence and mass gap problem—one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems \citep{MillenniumPrize}. This solution satisfies the rigorous mathematical criteria established for the prize, including the construction of a quantum field theory with a mass gap $\Delta_{\mathrm{UIDT}} > 0$ separating the vacuum from the lowest excited state, the demonstration that correlation functions decay exponentially at large distances, and the proof of asymptotic freedom through renormalization group analysis.

The theory was originally developed to address the Yang--Mills mass gap problem through information-theoretic principles, but extends naturally to cosmological scales through the introduction of a single dimensionless invariant $\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}} \approx 16.339$. This invariant emerges not as a free parameter fitted to observational data, but rather as a mathematical necessity arising from the self-consistent solution of three coupled nonlinear equations: a vacuum equation derived from extremizing the effective potential of the scalar field, a mass gap equation obtained from Schwinger--Dyson analysis of gluon propagators including back-reaction effects, and a renormalization group fixed point equation ensuring asymptotic safety at ultraviolet scales. The numerical value of $\gamma_{\mathrm{UIDT}}$ represents a universal scaling factor that relates quantum chromodynamic phenomena to gravitational and cosmological observables through information-theoretic principles.

Within this framework, dark energy arises naturally from the statistical variance of the information density field $S(x)$ integrated over cosmological horizon volumes. The effective cosmological constant is not truly constant but evolves proportionally to the variance of $S(x)$, which itself evolves with cosmic expansion according to thermodynamic properties of information encoded holographically on the cosmic horizon. We implement this connection through Barrow--Tsallis hybrid entropy \citep{Barrow2020,Tsallis2009}, a generalization of the Bekenstein--Hawking entropy formula that simultaneously incorporates fractal quantum gravity corrections (parametrized by a Barrow index $\Delta$) and non-extensive statistical mechanics effects (captured by a Tsallis parameter $\delta$). This hybrid entropy formalism provides the crucial link between quantum gravity effects at the Planck scale and observable cosmological dynamics at Hubble scales.

Dark matter, in our framework, emerges as stable thermodynamic configurations in the information density field rather than as a new species of weakly interacting particles. These configurations represent local enhancements of information density stabilized by entropy constraints, analogous to solitonic solutions in classical field theory but with stabilization mechanisms rooted in information-theoretic principles rather than conventional potential energy barriers. The phenomenology of these information-bound states naturally reproduces observed dark matter effects on galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, and structure formation, while simultaneously resolving tensions in small-scale structure observations such as the diversity of dwarf galaxy central density profiles.
